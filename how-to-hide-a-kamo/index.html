How to camouflage something nobody can find

Most hides get spotted in under three seconds, and almost always for the same handful of reasons. Here is what actually makes a kamo disappear — where to put it, what to point at, and the painting habits that give you away.

Pick the surface before you pick the spot

The single biggest factor is what’s behind the kamo, and you choose it before you place anything. A flat, evenly-lit wall is the hardest surface in the game: any colour you paint is either exactly right or obviously wrong, and the eye finds “obviously wrong” instantly.

What swallows a kamo is visual noise — surfaces where the eye is already losing track of edges:

  • Gravel, bark, moss, dry leaves
  • Patterned rugs, tweed, knitted blankets
  • Brick, concrete with staining, rusted metal
  • Bookshelves, cluttered desks, tangled cables
  • Dappled light under a tree

On a busy surface, a rough paint job reads as more texture. On a plain surface, a perfect paint job still reads as a shape.

Break the silhouette, not just the colour

People do not find your kamo by noticing the wrong shade of brown. They find it by noticing an outline. A kamo standing in open space with three inches of clearance around it is visible even when the colour match is perfect, because the human eye is a shape detector first and a colour detector second.

So put it where an edge already exists and let the two edges collide: against a skirting board, half behind a plant pot, in the seam between two paving slabs, in the corner where a wall meets a shelf. If part of the kamo's outline is interrupted by something real, the rest stops reading as a figure.

The shadow rule

Shadows cut both ways. A kamo in a shadow is easy: everything there is dark and low-contrast, so the match is forgiving. A kamo casting an obvious shadow — or painted in bright shades while sitting in shade — is the most common giveaway there is. Match the light level before you match the hue.

Paint the surroundings, not the figure

The instinct is to look at the kamo and decide what colour it should be. The better instinct is to look at the square inch of world it is standing on and copy that, patch by patch, onto whichever part of the figure is in front of it.

That means a kamo is rarely one colour. If its head is against a dark plank and its feet are against pale grout, painting the whole thing mid-grey gives you a mid-grey figure on a two-tone background — which is worse than either. Split it.

Cover the whole thing

A missed patch is worth more to a seeker than any amount of good work elsewhere. Unpainted areas keep their original colour, and that colour is designed to be visible. Rotate the view before you send: the bit you never painted is usually the bit you never looked at.

Zoom out before you're happy

Everything looks hidden at arm's length on your own screen, because you know where it is. The test that matters is the one the seeker takes: pull back so the kamo is a small part of a wider frame, then look for the thing that draws your eye first. If it's still your kamo, it isn't hidden — it's just painted.

Two more checks worth doing before you send a hide:

  • Squint. Blurring the detail collapses the image to blocks of value. A hidden kamo disappears when you squint; a visible one becomes a dot.
  • Wait a beat. Look away, look back. Your eye returns to the anomaly first.

Make it findable — eventually

A hide nobody can ever find isn't fun, it's just a photo. The hides people actually enjoy are the ones where the answer is there, in the frame, at a reasonable size, and the reveal makes them say "it was right there". Hiding a kamo three pixels wide behind a table leg wins the round and loses the game.

The best ones sit in the middle: findable in about twenty seconds of real looking, with a reveal that feels like a trick rather than a technicality.
